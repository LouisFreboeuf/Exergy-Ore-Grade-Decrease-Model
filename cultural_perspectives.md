### From Hierarchist and Egalitarian to Marginalist and Restorative Perspectives

In Life Cycle Impact Assessment (LCIA), the **Hierarchist (H)** and **Egalitarian (E)** perspectives originate from the cultural theory of risk. They represent different positions regarding the temporal extent, uncertainty, and precaution with which environmental impacts should be assessed.

- The **Hierarchist** perspective reflects a pragmatic position based on scientific consensus and commonly accepted time horizons.
- The **Egalitarian** perspective adopts a more precautionary approach, considering longer-term consequences and lower-probability effects.

In ReCiPe, for example, these perspectives are operationalized through different choices concerning time horizons and the inclusion of effects for which scientific evidence or consensus is more limited.

Although this distinction is useful for defining the **temporal and epistemic scope** of an assessment, it does not specify **which physical counterfactual should be used to characterize resource loss**. The two resource-impact formulations considered here reveal a different and more fundamental distinction.

---

#### The Marginalist Perspective

The first formulation considers the resource state immediately before the product-system-induced depletion. Its initial concentration ($x_i$) is therefore close to the final reference concentration ($x_f$), with their difference representing only the marginal decrease in ore quality attributable to the product system:

$$ERC_{\mathrm{marginal}} = k\left[b_c(x_f)-b_c(x_{i,\mathrm{marginal}})\right]$$

This formulation asks:

> **What additional effort will future society need because the present product system has marginally reduced the quality of the resource base?**

It therefore represents a **Marginalist Perspective**. The impact is an incremental future burden: present extraction changes the quality of the remaining resource and consequently increases the effort required for future extraction. This is conceptually consistent with surplus-energy, surplus-ore, and surplus-cost approaches to resource depletion.

---

#### The Restorative Perspective

The second formulation uses, as its initial state, the **actual concentration of the resource after dissipation**:

$$ERC_{\mathrm{restorative}} = k\left[b_c(x_f)-b_c(x_{i,\mathrm{dissipated}})\right]$$

where $x_{i,\mathrm{dissipated}} \ll x_f$. It therefore asks:

> **What effort would be required to restore the resource from the state into which the product system has dissipated it to a useful reference concentration?**

This represents a **Restorative Perspective**. Its concern is not the marginal deterioration of the natural resource base, but the loss of accessibility caused by the physical dispersion of the resource. It is closely related to the emerging LCIA literature on resource dissipation, but differs by expressing dissipation as an **exergy restoration requirement**.

---

#### Summarizing the Distinction

The distinction can consequently be summarized as:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Marginalist: future replacement of what has been appropriated}
}
$$

versus

$$
\boxed{
\text{Restorative: future restoration of what has been dissipated}
}
$$

---

#### Why the New Terminology?

The terms **Marginalist** and **Restorative** are preferable to **Hierarchist** and **Egalitarian** for these two characterization approaches because they identify the **mechanism and counterfactual represented by the equations**, rather than the broader ethical or epistemological stance of the assessment.

Nevertheless, the analogy with the Hierarchist–Egalitarian distinction is useful:

- The **Marginalist** perspective can be interpreted as closer to the pragmatic intuition of the Hierarchist perspective: it evaluates the **incremental consequence that future production is expected to experience**.
- The **Restorative** perspective is closer to the precautionary intuition of the Egalitarian perspective: it considers the **full physical consequence of an irreversible loss of accessibility**, even when that consequence may not correspond to the economic or technological response that future production systems will actually adopt.

---

#### A Complementary Framework

Thus, the proposed distinction should **not** be presented as a replacement of the Hierarchist and Egalitarian perspectives, but rather as a **more physically explicit second dimension of LCIA perspective**:

- **Hierarchist and Egalitarian** perspectives primarily determine *how far into the future and under what degree of precaution impacts are considered*.
- **Marginalist and Restorative** perspectives determine *which future state is used as the counterfactual reference for resource loss*.

This suggests that the two distinctions are potentially complementary:

$$
\boxed{
\text{LCIA perspective} = \text{temporal/epistemic stance} + \text{physical counterfactual}
}
$$

where the first dimension may be expressed through **Hierarchist–Egalitarian**, and the second through **Marginalist–Restorative**.

Under this interpretation, the Marginalist and Restorative perspectives are not simply new names for Hierarchist and Egalitarian perspectives. They constitute a **new, orthogonal distinction** that is required to describe the fundamentally different physical meanings of depletion and dissipation in resource LCIA.
